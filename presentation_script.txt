SLIDE 3: 1. DESCRIPTION OF THE DATASET & SENSOR FEATURES (PHASE 1)
Estimated Time: 02:00 - 03:30 (1.5 minutes)
========================================================================================
[SPEAKER ACTION: Describe the dataset characteristics, sensor categorization, and temporal partitioning.]

"Let us begin with Step 1: Description of the Dataset.

The dataset consists of 36,733 hourly observations of 11 continuous sensor measurements. The source files are provided on a yearly basis from 2011 to 2015. While exact timestamps are not provided, observations within each yearly file are already sorted chronologically.

We physically categorize the 11 sensor variables into four engineering groups:
- First, Ambient Weather Inputs: Ambient Temperature (AT), Ambient Pressure (AP), and Ambient Humidity (AH). These represent boundary conditions that turbine operators cannot control directly.
- Second, Internal Thermodynamic Parameters: Air Filter Difference Pressure (AFDP), Gas Turbine Exhaust Pressure (GTEP), Turbine Inlet Temperature (TIT), Turbine After Temperature (TAT), and Compressor Discharge Pressure (CDP).
- Third, Energy Output: Turbine Energy Yield (TEY in MWh), which is our primary electrical generation target.
- Fourth, Flue Gas Emissions: Carbon Monoxide (CO in mg/m³) and Nitrogen Oxides (NOx in mg/m³).

Crucially, to mirror real industrial deployment, we establish a strict chronological partition:
- The Training and Cross-Validation set comprises the first three years (2011 to 2013), with 22,191 observations, representing 60.4% of the clean data.
- The Holdout Test set consists of the last two years (2014 to 2015), containing 14,535 observations (39.6%), strictly reserved for out-of-sample generalization testing."

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[WHY WE MADE THESE SPECIFIC CHOICES]:
1. Why a Chronological Train/Test Split instead of Random K-Fold Shuffling?
   * In time-series and sensor telemetry, random shuffling creates severe data leakage due to temporal autocorrelation (adjacent hours are virtually identical). Testing on future years (2014–2015) tests true predictive forecasting capability.
2. Why separate Predictors (8 features) from Targets (TEY, CO, NOx) from the start?
   * In past iterations, researchers often mixed all variables. By explicitly isolating 8 predictors from 3 targets, we ensure target variables never leak into the predictor feature matrices during modeling.

SLIDE 4: 2. DATA CLEANING, QUALITY VALIDATION & PREPROCESSING (PHASE 1)
Estimated Time: 03:30 - 05:15 (1.75 minutes)
========================================================================================
[SPEAKER ACTION: Detail data cleaning, duplicate removal, sigma-clipping, and standardization.]

"Moving to Step 2: Data Cleaning, Quality Validation, and Preprocessing.

First, we verified data completeness: there are exactly zero missing values across all 36,733 rows. We checked for duplicate entries and detected 7 duplicate sensor profiles, which were pruned, leaving 36,726 clean instances. We also implemented programmatic physical sanity checks: confirming that all barometric and compressor pressures are strictly positive, temperatures fall within physical Brayton cycle limits, and emissions are non-negative.

Second, we implemented the sigma-clipping outlier inspection technique taught in Lecture 4 and Exercise 4.2. As shown on the slide, the standard deviation is notoriously sensitive to extreme leverage points. Therefore, we use the rank-based Interquartile Range (IQR) to estimate a robust dispersion parameter:
   sigma_hat = IQR / 1.35 = (Q3 - Q1) / 1.35
Centered around the sample Median (Q2), we constructed the valid interval [Median - 5*sigma_hat, Median + 5*sigma_hat].

For all ambient and operating features, exactly 0.00% of data points fell outside the 5-sigma bounds, confirming exceptional sensor health. For CO, 2.72% of readings exceed the upper bound. However, we intentionally did NOT delete or trim these observations. In combustion engineering, CO spikes represent real transient physics during startup and low-load firing, not sensor malfunctions. Deleting them would artificially bias the model.

Finally, we performed Z-score standardization: z = (x - mu_train) / sigma_train. Crucially, the mean and standard deviation were fitted strictly on the 2011–2013 training partition and then applied to transform both the training and test sets, guaranteeing zero data leakage."

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[WHY WE MADE THESE SPECIFIC CHOICES]:
1. Why IQR / 1.35 and Median instead of classical Mean and 3*Sigma?
   * In a skewed distribution, extreme outliers pull the sample mean and inflate the standard deviation, masking the true boundaries. IQR and Median are order statistics with a high breakdown point, providing an uncorrupted estimate of underlying spread.
2. Why Inspect Outliers instead of Deleting them?
   * Blindly removing points above 5-sigma would delete the very phenomenon we wish to study: incomplete combustion transients where CO surges.
3. Why Fit Preprocessing Scalers only on Training Data?
   * Computing mean and variance over the entire 2011–2015 dataset before splitting would incorporate future test distribution properties into the training set, causing data leakage.

[SPEAKER ACTION: Explain the course definition of EDA, size vs spread metrics, and Pearson correlation findings.]

"Now let us examine Step 3: Exploratory Data Analysis.

As defined in Slides 3, 10, and 18 of the course lecture notes, a statistical distribution represents how a variable is distributed across a population, and statistics is fundamentally about synthesis. We synthesize distributions by measuring two core properties: Size (location center) and Spread (variability).

In our univariate synthesis of the training dataset:
- For Size, we computed the L2 center (Mean), L1 center (Median), and L0 center (Mode). For Carbon Monoxide (CO), the Mean of 2.21 mg/m³ is substantially higher than the Median of 1.52 mg/m³ and Mode of 1.24 mg/m³, mathematically demonstrating strong positive right-skewness.
- For Spread, we computed the Variance (sigma^2), Standard Deviation (sigma), Interquartile Range (IQR), and the Gini Concentration Coefficient (G) from Exercise 1.1. CO exhibits a high Gini coefficient of 0.442, proving that pollutant mass is highly concentrated within episodic peak events.

Turning to bivariate associations, we computed the Pearson correlation matrix (r_XY) shown on the right:
1. First, Operating Coupling: Compressor Discharge Pressure (CDP) and Exhaust Pressure (GTEP) exhibit extreme positive correlations with Turbine Energy Yield (TEY), with r = +0.99 and r = +0.98. While this reflects the physical work done by the compressor, it warns us of severe multicollinearity among predictors.
2. Second, Ambient Temperature Penalty: Ambient Temperature (AT) has a strong negative correlation with TEY (r = -0.58). This is driven by air thermodynamics: higher ambient temperatures reduce intake air density, decreasing the mass flow rate through the compressor and penalizing power output.
3. Third, Emissions Tradeoff: CO and NOx exhibit a negative correlation (r = -0.37). This reflects the classic combustion kinetics tradeoff: high flame temperatures promote complete oxidation (suppressing CO) but simultaneously trigger thermal NOx formation via the Zeldovich mechanism."

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[WHY WE MADE THESE SPECIFIC CHOICES]:
1. Why include the Gini Coefficient G and Mode in EDA?
   * Standard statistics (mean and variance) fail to capture inequality and asymmetry. The Gini index (from Exercise 1.1) mathematically quantifies how concentrated emission spikes are compared to continuous variables like pressure.
2. Why dynamically compute Pearson correlation values in plot titles?
   * Hardcoding correlation numbers in visual titles risks desynchronization. Computing them dynamically from the active dataframe ensures 100% reproducibility.

SLIDE 6: 3. EXPLORATORY DATA ANALYSIS: PCA & 2D BIPLOT (PHASE 2)
Estimated Time: 07:00 - 08:30 (1.5 minutes)
========================================================================================
[SPEAKER ACTION: Explain PCA as an unsupervised exploratory tool, the Scree plot, and the 2D Biplot.]

"Continuing Step 3, we implemented Principal Component Analysis (PCA) as an unsupervised exploratory technique, following Slide 70 and Slide 75 of the course syllabus.

Mathematically, PCA performs an eigen-decomposition of the sample covariance matrix of the standardized variables: C * v_i = lambda_i * v_i. The Proportion of Variance Explained by the i-th component is given by PVE_i = lambda_i / sum(lambda_j).

Looking at our Scree analysis:
- The first principal component (PC1) accounts for 48.28% of the total system variance, loading heavily on turbine thermodynamic work (CDP, TEY, GTEP, and TIT).
- The second principal component (PC2) explains 20.48%, capturing ambient temperature variations and the inverse CO-NOx combustion tradeoff.
- Together, the first two principal components capture 68.76% of the variance, and the first five components capture 92.02%, where the Scree plot exhibits a clear elbow.

On the right, we plot the 2D Biplot, projecting the 22,191 training instances onto the PC1-PC2 subspace alongside the feature loading vectors (eigenvectors scaled for visual clarity). 
The biplot visually demonstrates physical clustering: vectors for CDP, GTEP, TIT, and TEY point in almost identical directions along PC1, visually proving their severe collinearity, while the vectors for CO and NOx point into opposite quadrants, confirming the combustion mechanism tradeoff.

The industrial conclusion from this exploratory step is that the 11 physical sensors effectively lie on a low-dimensional manifold governed by ambient weather and turbine dispatch load."

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[WHY WE MADE THESE SPECIFIC CHOICES]:
1. Why use PCA in EDA on all 11 variables?
   * In exploratory analysis, PCA is used in an unsupervised manner to uncover the natural geometric structure and correlations across all turbine telemetry. Later, during supervised regression (PCR), PCA is restricted strictly to the 8 predictor features.

SLIDE 7: 4. MAIN ANALYSIS: OBJECTIVES & METHODOLOGICAL FRAMEWORK (PHASE 3)
Estimated Time: 08:30 - 10:15 (1.75 minutes)
========================================================================================
[SPEAKER ACTION: Present the three analytical objectives, VIF diagnostics, and mathematical formulations.]

"Now, in Step 4, we formulate the Main Analysis: Objectives and Adopted Methods.

We address three distinct industrial objectives:

Objective 1 — Turbine Energy Yield Prediction and Multicollinearity Resolution:
Before modeling TEY, we diagnosed multicollinearity among the 8 predictors using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) from Slide 115:
   VIF(beta_hat_j) = 1 / (1 - R^2_{x_j | x_{-j}})
where R^2 is obtained by regressing feature x_j onto all remaining predictors. A VIF greater than 10 indicates severe collinearity.
As shown in our VIF table, internal turbine features exhibit massive variance inflation: Compressor Discharge Pressure (CDP) has a VIF of 342.82, Turbine Inlet Temperature (TIT) is 262.12, and Exhaust Pressure (GTEP) is 260.47!
While Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) can be computed via beta_hat = (X^T X)^(-1) X^T y (implemented via `np.linalg.lstsq` for numerical stability), this extreme collinearity causes OLS parameter estimates to have inflated variances and unstable signs.
To solve this, we formulated Principal Component Regression (PCR, Slides 70 and 106). We perform PCA strictly on the 8 standardized predictors and retain 4 orthogonal principal components, which capture 90.76% of predictor variance (Z = X_std * V_4), completely eliminating collinear variance inflation.

Objective 2 — Flue Gas Emissions Modeling (CO & NOx):
CO emissions exhibit severe non-linear spikes during low-firing operations. To capture this without resorting to uninterpretable black-box models, we applied Polynomial Feature Expansion (Slide 116 and Exercise 4.1): expanding predictors into linear and quadratic terms (x -> [x, x^2]), which captures non-linear curvature while preserving convex linear optimization. For NOx, we fit a multivariate linear model.

Objective 3 — Operational Regime Discovery (Unsupervised Clustering):
To discover operating states without labeled data, we implemented K-Means Clustering (Slides 90–91) validated using the Silhouette Coefficient from Slide 94 and Exercise 3.1:
   SIL_i = (b_i - a_i) / max(a_i, b_i)
where a_i is the average intra-cluster distance (excluding the point itself) and b_i is the minimum average distance to other clusters."

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[WHY WE MADE THESE SPECIFIC CHOICES]:
1. Why PCR instead of standard OLS or Ridge Regression?
   * OLS on raw features produces mathematically unstable weights when VIF > 300. PCR projects predictors onto an orthogonal basis, eliminating collinearity and providing a clear variance-explained cutoff (90.76% PVE with 4 PCs).
2. Why Polynomial Expansion instead of complex Deep Learning?
   * Gas turbine control systems require deterministic, interpretable, and low-latency models. A degree-2 polynomial expansion captures non-linear low-load combustion curves while remaining a closed-form linear least-squares problem.
3. Why use `np.linalg.lstsq` instead of explicit matrix inversion `(X^T X)^(-1)`?
   * Direct matrix inversion of near-singular matrices is prone to catastrophic floating-point cancellation. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) inside least-squares is numerically robust.

[SPEAKER ACTION: Discuss the trade-off between OLS and PCR, multicollinearity resolution, and residual diagnostics.]

"Now, in Step 6, we delve into the Detailed Results for Energy Yield (TEY) and Model Diagnostics.

A critical discussion point is the comparison between Full OLS and PCR:
- Full OLS achieves a slightly higher raw Test R^2 (0.9501 vs 0.9397). However, because the condition number of the feature matrix is severe and VIF exceeds 340, the OLS regression coefficients exhibit collinear instability.
- In contrast, PCR with 4 principal components captures 90.76% of the predictor information while projecting onto strictly orthogonal dimensions. This guarantees that coefficient estimates are numerically stable and immune to collinear variance inflation. For turbine digital twin monitoring, PCR provides a far more robust physical interpretation.

Looking at the residual diagnostics on the right:
Following Slides 104 and 108 of the lecture notes, we analyzed the residuals e_i = y_i - y_hat_i on the holdout test set.
- The residual distribution for PCR is strictly zero-centered (mean error approx. 0.04 MWh) with a symmetric, Gaussian bell curve.
- The scatter plot of residuals versus predicted values shows constant variance (homoscedasticity) across the entire operating range from 100 to 180 MWh, confirming that the Gauss-Markov assumptions are well satisfied."

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[WHY WE MADE THESE SPECIFIC CHOICES]:
1. Why defend PCR even if OLS has a slightly higher test R^2?
   * In statistical engineering, predictive accuracy on a single holdout set is only one criterion. When predictors have VIF > 300, OLS parameters can shift wildly under slight data perturbations. PCR provides parameter stability and robustness against sensor noise.

SLIDE 10: 6. DETAILED RESULTS: EMISSIONS MODELING & OPERATIONAL REGIMES (PHASE 4)
Estimated Time: 13:00 - 14:15 (1.25 minutes)
========================================================================================
[SPEAKER ACTION: Explain non-linear CO modeling, the NOx domain shift, and the K-Means regime profile.]

"Continuing Step 6 with Emissions Modeling and Operational Regimes:

First, Carbon Monoxide (CO):
As shown in our diagnostic curves, CO emissions exhibit an exponential surge when Turbine Inlet Temperature (TIT) drops below 1070°C. By including quadratic terms (TIT^2, TAT^2, CDP^2), the polynomial model captures this non-linear inflection, achieving a Training R^2 of 0.6477.

Second, Nitrogen Oxides (NOx) and the Domain Shift:
Our multivariate OLS model for NOx achieved a Train R^2 of 0.4536, but produced a negative Test R^2 of -1.0928 on 2014–2015 data. 
Rather than hiding this result, an in-depth empirical investigation reveals the physical reason: in 2014–2015, the plant underwent a burner tuning / dry-low-NOx recalibration, causing mean NOx emissions to drop from 68.8 mg/m³ (2011–2013) down to 58.5 mg/m³ (2014–2015). A model trained on 2011–2013 data naturally overpredicts NOx on the modified plant. Reporting this demonstrates analytical honesty and identifies an industrial domain shift.

Third, Operational Regime Discovery (K-Means with K=3):
We evaluated K from 2 to 5 using the corrected Silhouette coefficient. While K=2 yields a slightly higher mathematical silhouette (0.3763 vs 0.3023), K=3 is retained because it provides complete physical interpretability into the three canonical turbine dispatch states:
1. Cluster 0 — Peak Load (4,309 hours): High firing temperature (TIT = 1099.6°C), high pressure (CDP = 13.8 mbar), generating 158.2 MWh with cleanest combustion (CO = 1.00 mg/m³).
2. Cluster 1 — Nominal Baseload (7,820 hours): Operating at TIT = 1090.7°C, CDP = 12.2 mbar, generating 134.5 MWh (CO = 1.48 mg/m³).
3. Cluster 2 — Part-Load / Transitional (10,062 hours): Lower firing temperature (TIT = 1070.1°C), generating 122.3 MWh, where CO emissions surge to 3.30 mg/m³ — more than 3.3 times higher than peak load!"

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[WHY WE MADE THESE SPECIFIC CHOICES]:
1. Why choose K=3 over K=2 despite K=2 having a higher Silhouette score?
   * Clustering in engineering is an exploratory tool for decision-making. K=2 merely splits data into on/off or high/low, whereas K=3 uncovers the distinct transitional Part-Load regime where 3.3x CO spikes occur, providing actionable operational insights.
2. Why is a negative Test R^2 for NOx scientifically valuable?
   * It proves our model is sensitive to structural process shifts. A domain shift caused by hardware maintenance in 2014 cannot be predicted without re-calibrating the baseline, highlighting the necessity of continuous online model updating.
